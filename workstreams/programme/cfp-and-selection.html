CFP and selection#

Why it matters#

The call for proposals is where the program gets its content and where the community judges whether the conference is fair. A call that is slow, unclear or lopsided produces a thin program and bruised relationships.

The people affected are speakers, who decide whether to commit travel, the volunteers who review, sponsors who want visible content, and the Foundation, whose planning schedule sets the rhythm of calls and decisions. Without written criteria, selection turns into private judgment and disputes follow.

The three currencies are all at stake. Reviewers give weeks of unpaid time, a late or confusing call costs speaker trust, and a missing review step leaves the schedule chapter with nothing to build on. The sequence below feeds Scheduling and takes its scope from Programme portfolio.

Applies when#

  • all Every conference needs a way to gather and choose sessions.

Decisions you must make#

  • DEFAULT (basis: L-3126, L-1064) Does the hosting proposal say how the community will shape the agenda? Past hosting commitments describe an open call and a balanced program. The register records this as an expectation of proposals (R-027), but it rests on earlier calls only and is unverified as of 2026-11-02. Treat it as a strong recommendation until the Foundation confirms it. (decide-by: T-12m)

  • DEFAULT (basis: L-1066, L-2010, L-3038) Who makes the final selection? Let a named program lead or small group decide, with a committee that scores and advises. Screen and balance submissions before any public vote. (decide-by: T-9m)

  • OPTION Where are proposals submitted and scheduled? One organizer advises using the same system for both, to avoid copying text between systems (L-3037). (decide-by: T-9m)

  • OPTION What are the evaluation criteria and the scoring scale? Publish the criteria with the call. One committee rated proposal quality and presenter quality from 1 to 5 (L-1068). (decide-by: T-6m)

  • OPTION How long does the call stay open? One call ran three weeks, an online conference left it open until about six weeks before the event (L-1068, L-4167). (decide-by: T-6m)

  • DEFAULT (basis: L-2010, L-3038) Will attendees vote on proposals? If yes, screen submissions first and keep any interest poll clearly separate from the submission channel. Public votes produced duplicated topics at one conference and confusion at another. (decide-by: T-6m)

  • OPTION Is there a wait-list, and what do rejected proposers get offered? One team wait-listed a few sessions and offered rejected proposers a lightning talk or a birds-of-a-feather session (L-1069). (decide-by: T-3m)

How-to#

Before the call#

  1. Name a program lead and a committee of experienced community members and trusted local peers. Done: roster agreed and recorded.

  2. Write the evaluation criteria and the speaker compensation or ticket policy, and publish both with the call. Done: the criteria are on the call page. See community-and-speaker-expectations.

  3. Choose the submission system, preferably the one that will also build the schedule (L-3037; S026). Done: a test submission has been made and edited end to end.

  4. Build one call per product from cfp-template. Done: calls published with deadlines.

While the call is open#

  1. Ask people who submit World Plone Day talks to submit conference talks as well (L-4034; S004, as of 2026-11). Done: the message has gone out in April or at the nearest equivalent date.

  2. Ask well-connected community members to recruit strong proposals personally and to remind people of the deadline. Done: a weekly proposal count is posted against a target.

Review and decide#

  1. Screen proposals for duplicates, fit and completeness. Done: a screened list exists before any public vote.

  2. Have each committee member rate proposals within a fixed window, with reminders. Done: aggregated scores per proposal.

  3. Let the program lead build the selection from the scores and the portfolio goals. Done: a decision list with a reason for every rejection.

  4. Send acceptances. The Foundation planning schedule places review and acceptances in August, before the full schedule is published (L-4041; S004, as of 2026-11). Done: every proposer has an answer, a few sessions are wait-listed, and the list goes to Scheduling.

Principles and general lessons#

One principle has passed the promotion rule on a structural reason. Two further pieces of advice rest on a single conference each and are tracked here until independent evidence arrives:

  • Combine invited sessions with an open call for proposals (L-1061, S009).

  • Use a program committee to review proposals, but do not make it choose the sessions (L-1067, S009).

[general]

lesson | scope: general | source: S009, S004 | ledger: L-1065, L-4040 | confidence: observed | status: active | basis: structural | reason: Deadlines pull submissions toward the end, so supply must be prompted, whatever the year.

Do not wait for proposals to arrive. Recruit strong ones personally and chase the count against a target.

Risks#

Too few proposals arrive before the deadline

risk | likelihood: medium | impact: high | mitigation: Recruit proposals personally and track the count weekly against a target, and extend the call only with an announced revised deadline | owner-role: program lead

The Foundation schedule itself expects late submissions, so the count at the deadline understates the final total.

An accepted speaker withdraws after the program is published

risk | likelihood: medium | impact: medium | mitigation: Keep a short wait-list from the review and know in advance which slots a lightning talk or open space session can fill | owner-role: program lead | playbook: speaker-dropout

Judgment holds for a typical mid-size conference.

A public vote duplicates or distorts the program

risk | likelihood: low | impact: medium | mitigation: Screen and balance submissions before any vote and keep interest polling apart from submission | owner-role: program lead

Two conferences saw duplicated topics or voter confusion.
