Template: sponsor prospectus#

Copy the prospectus skeleton below and fill in the placeholders in angle brackets. Prices, tier names and benefits are yours to set from your budget. The decisions and the sponsor workflow are in Sponsors, and the income target comes from Finance.

What earlier prospectuses teach#

Four published tier structures appear in the record, and they agree on the shape.

  • Seattle 2006 had four tiers. Sponsors found the top package attractive but the difference between the middle and lower tiers unclear, and the organizers concluded that the value proposition needed more work (L-1038, L-1040). Tiers had been adapted from earlier conferences, and matching earlier levels was enough, so little outreach to first-time sponsors happened (L-1039).

  • Bristol 2010 advertised packages on the website. No top-tier sponsor signed, but many lower-tier sponsors compensated for the gap (L-2006).

  • Brasília 2013 bundled packages for two conferences, to reach sponsors of the co-located event who did not usually sponsor Plone (L-3113). Some public-body sponsors were expected to pay in services instead of cash, which lowers real income but offsets expenses (L-3119).

  • Tokyo 2018 offered five tiers priced in local currency, with a benefits matrix running from a name on the website to a booth, a company talk and party branding (L-4143).

Two further points shaped the template. The Foundation Board agreed a category for universities and local governments (L-4120), and one host listed individual contributors as a category beside company tiers (L-4128). A sponsor-owner who handles only sponsors, from first contact to the final thanks, keeps sponsors feeling they got their money's worth (L-3083).

Tier design rules#

  1. Offer three to five tiers. Make each tier differ from the next in at least one named benefit, which is the lesson from the unclear middle tiers (L-1040).

  2. Price in local currency, state whether VAT is included, and state the deadline for each tier.

  3. Put the benefits in a matrix, one row per benefit and one column per tier. A benefit that appears in every tier is not a benefit, it is a baseline.

  4. Add a category for public bodies, universities and individual contributors if your community wants one (L-4120, L-4128).

  5. State whether in-kind support counts toward a tier, and who values it (L-3119).

  6. Offer a bundle only when a real co-located event shares the audience (L-3113).

  7. Turn groups of attendees from one organization into sponsors with a targeted offer (L-3081).

  8. Write the number of expected participants as a range with its basis, and never as a promise. One prospectus expected about 200 participants and the event later reported about 150 (L-4142).

Claims you must be able to keep#

Every sentence in a prospectus becomes a promise.

  • Do not promise registrant contact data unless you collect opt-in consent. The Seattle team wished it had offered contact lists to top sponsors and did not, because no permission mechanism existed in time (L-1041). Under current EU data protection law, sharing registrant details with sponsors needs opt-in consent (L-1042).

  • Name sponsor passes in the tier, and count them in the ticket budget. Complimentary tickets were grossly underestimated at one conference (L-3012).

  • State exactly when each benefit is delivered, for example logo on the site by a date, talk slot after program freeze, party branding before print freeze.

  • Do not promise a booth, a room or a talk slot that your venue contract does not allow.

  • Do not use language implying Foundation endorsement unless the Foundation agrees.

Perk pattern: logo in the post-conference video#

Some conferences publish a thank-you or recap video after the event. A generic pattern for a perk that uses it:

  1. State the perk in the tier: "Your logo appears in the post-conference video." Limit it to tiers where the value is clear, and say which tier it starts at.

  2. Say in the prospectus what the video is and when it will be released, for example within a stated number of weeks after the event.

  3. Agree the logo format with the sponsor in the agreement, so you can use the supplied file without chasing.

  4. Collect logos in one place before the editing starts.

  5. Show every entitled sponsor in the video, in one card or sequence. Order them by tier, then alphabetically inside a tier.

  6. Check the finished video against the contract list before release, so no promised logo is missing.

  7. Send each sponsor a link with the thank-you message, as part of the report-back that funded events owe their supporters (L-4052).

Perk delivery patterns

thin | kind: single-source | needs: Archived accounts of how past conferences delivered sponsor perks, including video credits, and what sponsors reported back | who-might-know: Sponsor owners of past conferences; Conference Committee | area: sponsorship-down-market

The video perk pattern comes from one conference's practice and has no archived ledger row yet.

After the sale#

  1. Track every prospect, owner, next step and outcome in a sponsor tracker.

  2. Agree every sponsorship in writing before delivering any benefit (see Contracts, legal and insurance).

  3. Deliver from a checklist built from the signed agreements.

  4. Report back after the event with what was delivered, a thank-you and the photos you can share.

  5. Feed income and in-kind value into the budget sheet.
