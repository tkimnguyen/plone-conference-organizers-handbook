Template: call for proposals#

Copy the call text below once per product: talks, trainings, and any side track or lightning-talk slot. Edit the placeholders in angle brackets, delete what does not apply, and publish the result with a deadline. The decisions behind each block, such as who selects and how long the call stays open, are in CFP and selection.

What a good call does#

The 2006 Seattle call set the standard that later calls copy. It had to be open and fair, and it also had to move fast. It stated its evaluation criteria, suggested specific topics and announced the speaker compensation plan (L-1064). Those four elements, open, fast, criteria-first and compensation stated, are the skeleton of the call text below.

Four further habits are worth keeping:

  1. Combine invited sessions with an open call (L-1061).

  2. Ask a well-connected community member to recruit strong proposals personally and to remind people of the deadline (L-1065).

  3. Use one system for submission and for scheduling, so nobody copies text between systems (L-3037).

  4. Keep interest polling clearly separate from submission. At one conference a public poll for talk ideas was mistaken for the submission channel (L-3038).

Before you publish#

  1. Settle the decisions in CFP and selection: selection rule, criteria, scale, window and wait-list.

  2. Write the speaker compensation or ticket policy first, and say it in the call. Payment was kept deliberately limited at Seattle so the call would not feel high-stakes, and the organizers were glad of that choice when many worthy proposals had to be declined (L-1071).

  3. Name the committee by role, not by private contact.

  4. Run a test submission end to end and edit it once.

  5. Check the dates against the master timeline (see Master timeline).

Timeline guidance#

Calls need active chasing. The Foundation's planning schedule, as of 2026-11, aims for at least 75 percent of talks submitted by June for a September event and still expects some last-minute submissions (L-4040). It also suggests a message in April to people who submit local-day talks, asking them to submit conference talks too (L-4034).

Window lengths seen in the record:

  • Seattle's three-week window brought about 70 complete proposals, and committee members rated them within about a week after a few reminders (L-1068).

  • An online conference kept its call open until about six weeks before the event (L-4167).

Pick a window that leaves enough time to review, notify and schedule, and write the deadline in the call as a date, a time and a time zone.

The call text#

CALL FOR PROPOSALS: <Conference name and year>

<Conference name> takes place from <start date> to <end date> in <city>, <country>.
It brings together <one sentence about the audience, for example developers, integrators, users and decision makers>.
We welcome proposals from everyone, whether or not you have spoken before.

How to submit
Submit at <submission address> by <date, time, time zone>.
You can edit your proposal until the deadline.
Questions go to <role mailbox>.

What we are looking for
<One paragraph on the program shape: tracks, talk lengths, languages, level.>
Suggested topics, which are suggestions and not limits:
- <topic one>
- <topic two>
- <topic three>
- <topic four>
- <your topic>

What we offer speakers
<State the policy in plain words: ticket discount or free ticket, number of speakers per session
who qualify, travel or accommodation support if any, and what is not covered.>
If you need help with travel or a visa letter, write to <role mailbox> before <date>.

How we choose
A program committee of <size> experienced community members reads every proposal.
Each member rates the quality of the proposal and of the presenter from 1 to 5.
The program lead makes the final decisions, mostly following the committee's aggregated ratings.
We also balance the program across topics, levels, formats and speakers.
We will tell everyone by <notification date>.

Criteria
- Relevance to the people who attend
- Clarity of the proposal and the takeaway
- Evidence that the speaker can deliver it
- Originality, or a new angle on a known topic
- Fit with the rest of the program

What happens if we cannot accept your proposal
<State what you offer: wait-list, lightning talk, open-space slot or birds-of-a-feather session.>

Code of conduct
All speakers follow the code of conduct at <address>.
Slides and recordings: <state your policy on recording, license and publication>.

Dates
- Call opens: <date>
- Call closes: <date>
- Notifications: <date>
- Speaker confirmation: <date>
- Schedule published: <date>

Variant: call for trainings#

Training is a large draw and good marketing, and bad training harms the community. A conference co-sponsored by the Foundation should still run an open call for trainings so people feel involved, with a quality screen (L-3068). At one earlier conference there was no open call, to raise quality and limit the cost of cancelled rooms, and some community members felt shut out (L-3067).

Replace the "What we are looking for" and "Criteria" blocks with the following.

What a training must contain
- Hands-on exercises for participants on their own machines
- Stated prerequisites, so participants know what to bring and know
- Stated outcomes: what participants can do at the end
- Length: <half day or full day>
- Class size: <minimum and maximum>, and the rule for cancelling a session below the minimum

What we offer trainers
<Compensation, accommodation, equipment and room support, stated in writing before acceptance.>

Criteria
- Participants leave able to do something they could not do before
- The outline matches the stated level and prerequisites
- Evidence that the trainer has taught this material before

The three "must contain" items are the quality floor the 2026 analysis proposed after one attendee expected hands-on work and learned nothing (L-6022). Write them into the trainer agreement as well as the call.

Variant: lightning talks and side tracks#

Offer a lightning-talk or open-space slot as the second chance for declined proposers. At Seattle, rejected proposers were invited to give a lightning talk or convene a birds-of-a-feather session, and several did (L-1069). For a side track or a business track, add one line to the call stating its audience and its tone, so proposers do not pitch a developer talk to a decision-maker audience.

Review and scoring sheet#

Use the same scale for every reviewer, and keep review notes private.

PROPOSAL REVIEW: <proposal id>

Proposal quality, 1 (weak) to 5 (strong): __
Presenter quality, 1 (weak) to 5 (strong): __
Fits the program as it stands? yes / no / maybe
Overlap with another proposal? <id or none>
One sentence on why: ____
Conflict of interest to declare? yes / no

Screen and balance submissions before any public vote. Public voting on unscreened submissions produced duplicated topics at one conference, and the organizers concluded they should have balanced them first (L-2010).

After the decisions#

  1. Notify everyone on the date you promised, accepted or not.

  2. Keep a wait-list in case a speaker cancels. Four sessions were wait-listed at Seattle and none canceled (L-1069).

  3. Publish the schedule, and hand the accepted proposals to Scheduling.

  4. Save the call text, the scores and the decisions for the post-mortem (see Template: post-mortem).
