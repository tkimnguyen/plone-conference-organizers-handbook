Defining success#

Why it matters#

Without a written definition, success shrinks to a headcount, and headcounts are easy to inflate and hard to compare. A team that budgets on the largest numbers in the history of the conference plans for an audience that the recurring community may not supply.

The 2026 debrief described the recurring in-person community as roughly one hundred people and potentially shrinking (S098, as of 2026-11). That figure is an approximate planning hypothesis from one source, and the record does not yet hold comparable per-edition counts. The Foundation's history page also shows the difficulty: it counts online-stream viewers next to on-site attendees for some years, and its two largest in-person editions were co-hosted with a regional Python conference (S002, as of 2026-11).

A definition of success written at the start protects the team and the next one. It tells the community what the event is for, gives the treasurer a number to plan on, and lets the survey show whether goals were met.

Applies when#

  • all Every conference needs goals, an attendance baseline and a way to learn afterward.

Decisions you must make#

  • DEFAULT (basis: L-1018, L-2055) Do you write down what the conference is for before you build the program? Seattle fixed four goals before it built a program, and Bristol stated its goals in the bid. (decide-by: T-12m)

  • DEFAULT (basis: L-4089, L-4142, L-5010) What planning number do you use for the recurring community, kept apart from local and partner audiences? Forecasts in 2007 and 2018 ran above the reported outcome. (decide-by: T-12m)

  • OPTION Will you co-locate with a partner event? Both 2024 and 2025 did (L-4214, L-4215). It adds attendance and talks, and it blurs the measure of the community. (decide-by: T-12m)

  • OPTION Will you set a goal for local activation, and who owns it? A 2009 host company noted that few local users attended and called a local meetup afterward (L-4109). (decide-by: T-9m)

  • DEFAULT (basis: L-1019, L-4183, L-4218) Will you survey attendees and publish the results? Editions in 2006, 2023 and 2025 each reported survey results. (decide-by: T-6m)

  • OPTION Will you count remote viewers, and report them apart from on-site attendees? The history page lists both and does not make them comparable (L-4021). (decide-by: T-6m)

How-to#

Set the definition#

  1. Write three to five goals in plain language, for example who the event teaches, whom it connects and what it shows. Done looks like a one-page statement that the team and its advisers have read.

  2. Attach one measure to each goal that you can collect, such as a count, a survey question or an outcome. Done looks like a table with a goal and a measure per row.

  3. Estimate the recurring community separately from local and partner audiences, and write down the method. Done looks like two numbers in the budget, not one.

  4. Define what you count: on-site attendees, speakers, sponsors, sprinters, remote viewers. Done looks like a definitions line that travels with every published number.

Measure and report#

  1. Draft the survey before registration opens, with questions tied to your goals and not only an overall score. Expect roughly one in five ticket holders to answer, and pick measures that a response of that size can still answer (L-6002). Done looks like a survey that maps question by question to the measures.

  2. Send the survey right after the conference and set the publication date before you send it. Done looks like a dated results post.

  3. Have someone challenge your reading of the survey before you publish it. In 2026, three review rounds found two interpretations that the data contradicted and corrected several counts (L-6005). Done looks like a reading that names the evidence behind each claim.

  4. Publish the counts with their definitions and the composition of the audience. Done looks like an entry in the conference-data-table format.

  5. Hand the goals, numbers and survey to the next team through the handover-contract. Done looks like files in the handover package.

Principles and general lessons#

[general]

lesson | scope: general | source: S069, S079, S086 | ledger: L-1019, L-4183, L-4218 | confidence: reported | status: active | basis: independent-conferences

Survey attendees after the conference and publish the results. The editions on record that did so reported high ratings.

[general]

lesson | scope: general | source: S002, S077, S079, S098 | ledger: L-4021, L-4214, L-4215, L-5010 | confidence: reported | status: active | basis: independent-conferences

Estimate the recurring community apart from local and partner audiences. Attendance added by a partner event or by reduced local tickets does not show how large the community is.

[general]

lesson | scope: general | source: S010, S055, S057 | ledger: L-4080, L-4089, L-4142 | confidence: observed | status: active | basis: independent-conferences

Bid and prospectus numbers ran above the reported outcome in 2007 and 2018, and the Foundation lowered its venue minimum from 250 to 200 for 2018. Plan the budget on the lower figure.

A pre-bid community survey that collected the wisdom of past conferences and gave one team a clear idea of success (L-1016) comes from one conference and is not promoted.

Risks#

The budget rests on an attendance forecast the community does not reach

risk | likelihood: high | impact: high | mitigation: Budget on the recurring-community number and set dated sales checkpoints with a decision for each | owner-role: treasurer | playbook: low-ticket-sales

Bids and prospectuses ran above the outcome at several editions. The judgment is for a typical mid-size conference.

Partner-event attendance is read as community growth

risk | likelihood: medium | impact: medium | mitigation: Publish every attendance figure with its definition and split partner-event attendance from community attendance

A later team that reads the raw number plans for an audience that does not return.
