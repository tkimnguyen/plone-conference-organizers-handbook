# The problem history

Every Plone conference is organized by a team that then disbands.
The Plone community has held a conference each year since 2003, and each year a different company or group took the work on.
This page tells what the archive shows about how that knowledge passed from one team to the next, and what it shows about the cost of the gaps.
It is the reason the handbook exists, and the reason it comes with a yearly ritual and not only a text.

The account is as of 2026-11-02 and rests on the sources in the archive.
Where the archive is silent, the page says so.
Silence in the archive does not prove that nothing was handed over in private, and the page does not claim that.

## 2003 to 2005: nothing located

The archive holds no organizer account for the first three conferences.
The conference data table has dashes for those years, and the Foundation's own conference history page, as archived in 2026, lists editions only up to 2015 (L-4020).
Later editions are documented on separate yearly sites and in scattered news items, so there is no single place to find them.
A team that wants to learn from the early years starts by hunting for what exists.

## 2006: the exception

In 2006 the host organization in Seattle did something that no later edition repeated.
It published its whole evaluation on one public page: the pre-conference survey, the aggregated post-conference survey, the open-ended attendee comments, the detailed budget and a narrative lessons document addressed to future organizers (L-1003).
The lead organizer wrote that document after the event, with input from the co-organizer, the host's finance staff and community advisers, and stated its purpose as passing the host's experience on to future Plone event organizers (L-1004).

It was candid about money and mistakes, and it was written for the next team.
Even this account had one weak spot.
Per-session evaluation results were kept private and offered to future organizers on request, and no public record shows that anyone ever asked (L-1005).
An offer with no named recipient and no date tends to lapse.

The handbook takes the Seattle document as its skeleton.
The annotated digest in the appendices shows what still holds and what has aged.
The page `seattle-2006-digest` carries it.

## 2010 to 2012: scattered summaries and a second attempt

Bristol 2010 published summaries of its own, and an outside observer singled them out as exceptionally valuable (L-2069).
A reader of the Bristol account wrote that it would be nice to collate the various organizers' experiences into one conference organizers guide (L-2032).
The archive records the wish, and it records the next written attempt a year later.

San Francisco 2011 produced that attempt.
The organizers left a thirteen-page wiki guide for their successors, covering budget, tickets, volunteers, talks, scheduling, print, nonprofit status, the domain handover and a supplies list (L-3001).
It is one of the few written handovers in the whole series.
It was then left alone.
The wiki index shows no page edited after January 2012, two of the thirteen pages could not be captured in the archive, and nobody maintained it after the first handover (L-3002).
A good document without an owner, a date and a recurring occasion to update it decays in months.

Even a good handover had its own limits.
The 2011 team found that nearly everything took five times longer than expected (L-3074), and the conference email routing and the domain still had to be transferred at handover because they sat with one person (L-3048, L-3063).

## 2014 to 2023: the Foundation's calls become the memory

The Foundation's 2015 call for hosting proposals asked bid teams to read past organizers' lessons-learned reports and earlier winning proposals before writing their own, which made those documents the de facto handover material (L-4065).
That was a hopeful step and also a sign that no better handover text existed.
The calls listed requirements, and they did not collect what teams had learned.

The Foundation also acted as financial agent on a trial basis for San Francisco 2011, then for a 2014 regional symposium and for Bucharest 2015 and Boston 2016, and made it a standing offer from 2018 (L-4057).
The handover of financial know-how therefore moved into a policy, which is better than nothing and still not a record of lessons.

The official record of some editions is thin.
The Foundation's 2019 record holds a one-line note and links, with no recap, figures or lessons (L-4162).
The 2023 organizer recommended that the Foundation provide written guidelines for future conferences (L-4190).

## 2025: the Foundation says no list exists

In 2025 the Foundation board named a written list of requirements for organizing conferences and sprints among its priorities (L-4060).
Naming it as a priority confirms that no such list existed.
Requirements lived in resolutions, in calls for proposals, in a planning schedule and in committee habit, and teams met them as unwritten expectations.

This handbook's requirements register is an attempt to write that list down.
Each row cites its source and carries the date it was last checked, and the rows the handbook could not verify are marked as questions for the Foundation.
The register is on the page [Foundation requirements register](../evidence/requirements-register.md).

## 2026: the ambition that never started

The 2026 organizers proposed a documentation effort built on the docToolchain tool.
It never started, and a folder of Markdown files became the working archive instead (L-5039).
The lesson the team drew is that documentation maintenance has to match the team's daily working habits, because tooling ambitions beyond the available capacity can prevent the handover record from being written (L-5040).

The same team found how much of the job had been expected and never written down.
Its debrief records that unwritten assumptions can create obligations that first-time organizers have never budgeted for (L-5019).
Weeks after the conference, the Foundation's own record of it still described it as upcoming (L-4232).
The pattern in 2026 resembles the pattern in 2011: the work gets done, and whether an account of it reaches the next team depends on someone finding the time to write it.

## What the pattern costs

The archive shows the cost in specific places.
A 2011 team found that nearly everything took five times longer than expected (L-3074).
A 2026 debrief names trainer accommodation as an implicit expectation and the largest financial risk, and advises settling it before the training offer is confirmed (L-5020).
Money, time and trust are what is lost, and a community that relies on volunteers cannot afford to lose them each year.

## Why this handbook

The history suggests three causes, and the handbook answers each with a mechanism.

- **No owner.**
  A handover document without an owner decays, as the 2011 wiki did.
  The handbook names a steward and a deputy for each page and proposes a governance model; see [Governance](../running/governance.md).
- **No recurring occasion.**
  Teams write only when someone asks, and nobody asked.
  The handbook ties its update to the handover itself: the incoming team leads a yearly update with the outgoing team, on a fixed sequence of dates; see [The annual ritual](../running/annual-ritual.md).
- **No honesty about gaps.**
  A document that looks complete hides what is missing.
  The handbook publishes a thin registry that names each weak spot and what would close it; see [About the thin registry](../running/thin-registry-about.md).

Behind these sits one more mechanism, the lessons ledger.
Every lesson cites a row, every row has a source and a disposition, and every claim ages in public, so a reader can tell a twenty-year-old account from a recent one.
The handover contract in [Handover contract](../job/handover-contract.md) lists what an outgoing team owes the next one, including a list of what was expected and never written down.

## What this page does not claim

- It does not claim that earlier teams failed.
  They ran the conferences, and the community has been served by them for twenty years.
- It does not claim that nothing was handed over.
  The archive shows only what was published.
- It does not claim that the handbook will fix the pattern.
  It claims only that the pattern is visible as of 2026-11, and that the mechanisms above give it a chance.

The weakest point of the plan is the one the history already shows: documents without a recurring owner decay.
That is why the governance page is a proposal that asks the community to accept it, and why this handbook invites its readers to say so if they think it will not hold.
