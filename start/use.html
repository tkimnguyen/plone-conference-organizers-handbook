Use: your first 90 days#

This walkthrough shows the handbook in use from the day your bid is accepted to the day your first public sales start. It follows the 2026 team in Maastricht as the worked example, so you can see which page you open, what you decide, and what you write down at each step. The advice is general; the example is one conference, and your numbers will differ.

The loop is the same at every step.

  1. Open the chapter for the topic.

  2. Read the decisions you must make, and answer each as a yes, a no or a name.

  3. Follow the how-to and copy the templates from the appendices.

  4. Record the choice in your Tailoring Record; see Tailor.

The example uses no private figures. Where the 2026 team measured something, the chapters hold the generalized version with its sources. Each step names the evidence by topic, and the chapters show it in their case notes.

Days 1 to 7, the bid is accepted#

Pages to open. bid-and-selection, stakeholder-map, defining-success, community-and-speaker-expectations.

What the 2026 team did. The team started from the honest attendance picture: the community brings about a hundred attendees to a conference, the number tends to shrink, and earlier editions raised their numbers with local activation. The 2026 plans for local activation, two university outreach ideas, were never carried out. The handbook therefore asks you to turn local activation into an explicit scope decision with an owner and a drop-by date, instead of an idea that lingers on the board. Read the stakeholder map in the same week, to see who must be told, who must approve and who can say no.

What the team learned about expectations. The community-and-speaker-expectations chapter lists the implicit rules that nobody had written down: trainer rooms and travel as a large financial exposure, unregulated trainer compensation, speaker ticket discounts, and a party that nobody is entitled to but everyone values. Read it in week one, because these items shape the budget.

Done looks like. A one-page statement of success and a list of stakeholders with a named contact role for each.

Days 8 to 21, form the team#

Pages to open. team-and-governance, volunteers-and-roles, role-descriptions-template, master-timeline.

What the 2026 team did. It ran its work through issues and a project board, and wrote a day-in-the-life description for each role. A review of the 2026 issues and board found that this method works when three preconditions hold.

  • Issues are closed as the work finishes.

  • One board holds all the work, not several.

  • MUST items are checklist gates, so nobody can skip them unnoticed.

The master timeline gives every role its T-minus dates; the 2026 team ran a countdown plan with gates and owners in the last weeks.

Done looks like. A team list by role, a board with the first issues, and a timeline with owners.

Days 15 to 30, budget tooling comes first#

Pages to open. finance, budget-sheet-template, hledger-accounting-setup, legal-entity-and-financial-agent.

What the 2026 team did. It built a plain-text accounting setup: a living budget that converges on actuals, a shadow ledger, and import rules for the ticket platform and the payment provider. The tooling worked well, but it was stood up far too late, only in the final weeks before the event. The lesson is the order of work: budget tooling comes at the start, not when the first numbers are already moving. The same weeks exposed a second surprise. The selling entity decides the VAT regime, and the VAT regime changes the price a participant pays. An organizer in one country and a company taxed in another produced real cross-border VAT complexity, and refund questions reached the team during the sales campaign.

Why this order. With the budget tooling in place at the start, every sponsor payment, deposit and ticket sale is booked the way you will report it later. Settling who sells the ticket before you set prices avoids rewriting prices during sales.

Done looks like. A budget sheet with a scenario for low, expected and high attendance, a named financial agent, and a booking system that has received its first test entry.

Days 30 to 60, the call for proposals and the first sponsors#

Pages to open. programme-portfolio, cfp-and-selection, cfp-template, sponsors, sponsor-prospectus-template.

What the 2026 team did. It treated the parts of the event as separate products: trainings, the main conference, a parallel business track and sprints. Each product has its own audience, price and promise. The chapter on expectations warns that speakers expect a reduced ticket and that speakers make up a large share of participants, so settle speaker compensation and ticket rules before you open the call.

Done looks like. A published call with a deadline and selection criteria, a sponsor prospectus with packages, and a rule for how sponsor promises are tracked.

Days 45 to 90, tickets#

Pages to open. ticketing-and-registration, privacy-gdpr, web-comms-brand, marketing-and-outreach.

What the 2026 team did. It sold tickets through a hosted platform and learned to treat the platform fee as a real cost line; a self-hosted alternative with invoice or bank-transfer payment is documented as an option. It added a global-south ticket path and sought grant funding for it, and the argumentation is reusable. The community buys late, so the cash-flow plan has to expect that most revenue arrives close to the event. A defect in the schedule search of the conference website surfaced only a day before the event, so the handbook asks for a website quality gate weeks before.

Done looks like. A live registration page, a privacy notice that matches the data you collect, and a cash-flow forecast that shows the lowest point before the event.

The freeze dates pattern#

Last, the team fixed three freeze dates for the final weeks and wrote them into its countdown plan. They fell about two and a half weeks, two weeks and ten days before the first conference day.

Freeze

What stops changing

Why

Program freeze

The schedule and the speaker list

Print, signage and the website depend on it.

Numbers freeze

Final attendance and catering counts

Venue, catering and merch quantities depend on them.

Print and orders freeze

Artwork, badges, shirts and bags

Printers need lead time, and delivery needs a buffer.

Copy the pattern, not the dates. Count back from your own first day and add the lead time of your slowest supplier. The page master-timeline explains how to set the dates, and t-minus-checklist ties them to chapter anchors.
