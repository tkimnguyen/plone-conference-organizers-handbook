About and purpose#

This handbook is a public, community-maintained manual for the teams that organize a Plone conference. It collects what earlier organizers learned, so that the next team does not start from nothing. Both spellings, organizers and organisers, lead here.

What the handbook promises#

After you read the paths that fit your role, you know five things.

  • What you must decide.

  • By when you must decide it.

  • About what it costs.

  • What can go wrong.

  • What earlier conferences learned about it, with sources you can check.

The handbook does not run your conference for you. It is not a project management tool, not a booking system and not a substitute for local legal, tax or insurance advice.

Who it is for#

The primary reader is a team, usually one company or a small group, that is considering or running a Plone conference. The country and the size do not matter. Secondary readers are the Plone Foundation and the Conference Committee, who set requirements and oversee the event, and individual role owners such as the treasurer, the programme chair and the volunteer coordinator. The page Choose your path sends each of them to the right chapters.

The problem it answers#

The Plone community has held a conference every year since 2003. Each year a different company or group organizes it. Two attempts produced a formal account of lessons learned.

  • Seattle 2006 published Things We Learned About Running a Plone Conference, with its evaluation pages, survey results and public budget. It is the most complete account and the historical skeleton of this handbook.

  • San Francisco 2011 produced a wiki guide for organizing a Plone conference.

Every other year left little behind: scattered recaps, a few organizer blog posts, and the Foundation's calls for proposals, which turned some lessons into formal requirements. Each team reinvented the wheel and repeated mistakes that earlier teams had already paid for. Much of what an organizer needed to know was expected by the community and the Foundation but never written down.

The 2026 team in Maastricht built a working archive of its own preparation and then asked a plain question: how does the next team inherit this? This handbook is the answer. It is the product, and the processes in Part 4 keep it alive after the 2026 team has moved on. The longer account, with the evidence per year, belongs on the page problem-history.

Honesty statement#

A handbook that hides its weak spots is worse than no handbook. These are the limits, stated up front.

Advisory, not policy. The handbook is advice from organizers to organizers. It is not Plone Foundation policy and it is not legal, tax or insurance advice. Only items badged MUST carry the weight of a Foundation requirement or a legal necessity, and each of them cites the Foundation requirements register with an as-of date. How the badges work is explained in How to read lessons.

Survivorship bias. Every source in the evidence base comes from a conference that happened, mostly from a funded company or an established event. Conferences that never got started, were cancelled or ran on a tiny volunteer budget left almost no written record. The advice therefore leans toward mid-size, adequately funded, in-person events. A small-budget variant is planned as a stub, and the handbook says so wherever the lean matters.

Recency and place. Most evidence comes from Europe and North America. Sources from the 2020 and 2021 online editions are thin. Specific lessons show their age and place on the page, so you can discount them yourself.

What we do not know. Ten areas are declared thin in advance and marked on the pages that touch them.

  • Hybrid and online formats.

  • Accessibility.

  • Legal, tax and visa questions outside the EU and the United States.

  • Sponsorship in a weak market.

  • How ticket demand responds to price.

  • Small-budget and volunteer-run conferences.

  • Health, safety and safeguarding.

  • Sustainability.

  • Jurisdiction annexes, which all start as stubs.

  • The 2026 post-mortem, which is a marked draft until the team finishes it.

Who wrote it. The 2026 Maastricht organizing team drafted the first version with agent assistance. Every claim traces to a source or to a ledger row that cites one. Stewardship is a proposal that the wider community has not yet accepted; see Governance.

Where to see what is covered#

The coverage map is generated from every page's maturity and every thin marker. It shows which chapters are stubs, drafts, reviewed or proven, and how many thin markers each of the ten areas carries. A row with zero markers is shown on purpose, so a missing marker is visible. The page is coverage-map. The thin registry lists each marker with what would close the gap; see About the thin registry.

The four things you can do with it#

  • Use it to run your conference: Use: your first 90 days.

  • Tailor it to your size, country and budget model: Tailor.

  • Update it with what you learn: Update.

  • Extend it with chapters, annexes and lessons: Extend.
